# Exploit broadcast structure before training bigger models

The best value per effort comes from changes that use structure the broadcast and the pipeline already contain, not from heavier models. Five changes lead. Reading the score bug timestamps every made basket. A persistent rim box, moved with the camera, survives pans. Shot-cut detection stops replays and cuts from corrupting state. A stricter kit-split rule stops per-frame colour noise from splitting tracks. And the team and jersey labels the pipeline already votes on become cannot-link constraints for an offline GTA-style tracklet stitcher. Each takes hours to a day, needs no hand labels, and runs on CPU or in minutes on a T4. Next come 1–2-day fine-tunes on in-domain data (ball and rim from this venue, PARSeq per sport), pitch and court registration, and a single 4-class 1280-px YOLO26m/YOLO11m TensorRT FP16 engine in place of three detectors. Temporal ball models (WASB), learned event spotting (T-DEED/E2E-Spot) and full camera calibration (PnLCalib) come last. The published evidence supports the direction more firmly than the size of the gains. GTA added **4.7–6.3 HOTA and 9.5–12.4 IDF1** to a motion-only tracker ([GTA](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.08216v1)). In-domain PARSeq fine-tuning added **6.9 points** of jersey accuracy ([Koshkina & Elder](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.13896)). Ultralytics' T4 latencies imply the detector swap cuts GPU time from roughly **125 ms to 19 ms per frame** ([Ultralytics](https://docs.ultralytics.com/compare/yolov8-vs-yolo11/)). Turning on Ultralytics ReID will not dent the 694 football identities. BoT-SORT lets appearance lower the cost only for boxes that already overlap, so it can never re-link a player who left a panning frame ([bot_sort.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ultralytics/ultralytics/main/ultralytics/trackers/bot_sort.py)). The licence risk sits in weights and data, not in code. SportsMOT-trained ReID weights and PL-NBA are non-commercial, and SoccerNet video sits behind an NDA. Koshkina's jersey pipeline is CC BY-NC 3.0, SAM3 has a custom licence that bans some uses, and NBJW is GPL-2.0-only. Every code component on the recommended path is MIT, Apache-2.0, BSD or GPL-family code compatible with AGPL-3.0. None of these gains has been measured on footage like this, so build a small TrackEval harness and score-bug shot ground truth alongside the first fixes.

## Five label-free fixes top the value-per-effort ranking

The ranking weighs how much of a measured weakness each change can remove, or what it unlocks for other stages, against the days it costs. Gains that rest on weak evidence count for less. None of the top five needs a single hand label. They use what the footage and pipeline already supply: the on-screen score graphic, a rim that never moves, the cuts in a broadcast edit, and team and jersey labels already voted per track. The middle of the table holds registration and three 1–2-day fine-tunes on in-domain data, where most of the cost is labelling. The tail holds learned components that take several days and have no published result on comparable footage. Effort figures are estimates for a developer who knows the codebase. Several well-known options rank below the line. Online ReID on its own fails for the structural reason explained below. TensorRT INT8 runs only **0.90–1.20× as fast as FP16** at the median on newer GPUs and costs 3.4–7.6 mAP50-95 points ([Ultralytics benchmark](https://www.ultralytics.com/blog/onnx-vs-tensorrt)). That is a poor trade when the ball is already the weakest class. SAM2 mask tracking is what holds Roboflow's basketball pipeline to 1–2 FPS on a T4 ([Roboflow](https://roboflow.com/blog/identify-basketball-players)). The McByte mask tracker reaches 76.5 SportsMOT HOTA, but it needs SAM and Cutie and has no reported T4 runtime ([roboflow/trackers](https://github.com/roboflow/trackers)). SAM3 is gated, carries a custom licence, needs Python 3.12 and CUDA 12.6, and has no T4 benchmark ([SAM3](https://github.com/facebookresearch/sam3)). Projecting the rim from a floor homography, NBJW, AutoShot and the Koshkina codebase drop out for reasons given in the sections below.

### Measure before trusting a falling identity count

Identity fixes carry a trap: a stitcher that wrongly merges teammates lowers the identity count just as a correct one does. Counts still work as regression signals. The football clip creates about 69 identities a minute, roughly 28 for each of the ~25 people on the pitch. The basketball quarter creates about 88 for each of the 13 people on court. Only labelled frames can tell fewer fragments from wrong merges, and the tooling is free. TrackEval computes HOTA (with its detection and association parts, DetA and AssA), CLEAR MOT and IDF1 from MOTChallenge-format files. It needs only numpy and scipy, is MIT-licensed, and advises `--DO_PREPROC False` for custom data. It scores MOT17-train in **9.64 s on one core**, against 66.23 s for older toolkits ([TrackEval](https://github.com/JonathonLuiten/TrackEval)). roboflow/trackers offers the same metrics under Apache-2.0 ([roboflow/trackers](https://github.com/roboflow/trackers)).

A two-part labelling design keeps the budget small. Dense ground truth comes from two or three 60-second windows per sport, about 1,500 frames each, chosen to contain exits and re-entries. Annotate them in CVAT's track mode, which interpolates boxes between keyframes ([CVAT](https://docs.cvat.ai/docs/annotation/manual-annotation/modes/track-mode-basics/)). Long-range ground truth comes from identity labels at 1 fps across the whole clip, the rate the TAO benchmark uses ([TAO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.10356.pdf)). Score predictions only on those frames, and AssA and IDF1 then measure identity across the off-screen gaps that cause the fragmentation. The sparse protocol cannot see a swap and swap-back between two sampled frames, and no study measures its bias on multi-object metrics. The closest evidence comes from single-object tracking: 10 fps labels moved success AUC by only 0.237% relative to 30 fps ([LaGOT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.11920)). SportsMOT-val is the natural public set for tuning thresholds, and its CC BY-NC 4.0 licence permits that internal use. It forbids redistribution, though, and its clips average 485 frames with no shot changes ([SportsMOT](https://github.com/MCG-NJU/SportsMOT)), so it under-represents long off-screen gaps. SoccerNet-Tracking includes a full-half sequence for long-term evaluation ([SoccerNet-Tracking](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.06918)), but it sits behind an NDA ([SoccerNet](https://pypi.org/project/SoccerNet)). No source reports CVAT annotation rates for sports tracking, so the labelling budget is an estimate.

## Twelve missed baskets are a perception gap that venue labels close

The pipeline's own measurements locate the failure. The rule-based shot logic works whenever the ball is visible. But the detector never found the ball near the rim in the 5 s before 12 of 14 made field goals. It saw the rim in only 4–57% of frames, and upscaled hoop crops recovered nothing. That rules out object size and points to appearance shift. A low stands camera sees the rim almost edge-on against the crowd, unlike the NBA broadcast frames the YOLO11s was tuned on. Tiling and higher resolution will not help; in-domain data will. Ultralytics' own guidance says training images "must be representative of deployed environment" and that "partial labeling will not work". It adds that 0–10% background images help suppress false positives ([Ultralytics](https://docs.ultralytics.com/yolov5/tutorials/tips_for_best_training_results/)). Two fixes that take hours should come first, because they also produce the labels.

**Reading the score bug is the cheapest high-value change in the whole pipeline.** Finding baskets by OCR of the broadcast score overlay goes back at least to Bettadapura and colleagues' 2016 highlight generator for 25 NCAA games ([Bettadapura et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.08955)). A patent splits sports broadcasts into chapters at each scoreboard change, recording what changed and its old and new values ([US 8,606,090](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8606090)). A 2026 Lund thesis runs its deep basketball analysis only when the score changes, and inspects the 10 s before it ([Bian & Zhou](https://lup.lub.lu.se/student-papers/record/9236679)).

The recipe has four steps. Crop the bug's fixed region and read it at 1–2 fps. Read digits with a template matcher or small classifier fitted to the broadcaster's font, because plain Tesseract struggles on textured, shaded score graphics ([IIIT Hyderabad](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1801.01430)). Require the same reading over several samples in a row. Emit events on +2 and +3 changes (+1 for free throws), ignoring decreases and frames without the bug. Calibrate the scorer's update lag on the 14 known makes.

Each event does three jobs at once. It is **exact ground truth for a made basket**. It drops the 10 s before the score change into the labelling queue, and those are exactly the frames where the detector fails today. And it is a direct make signal, so vision only has to find the shooter and the release. Missed shots still need vision. The NBA datasets that line up play-by-play with video by game clock use the NBA API and NBA.com clips ([BasketEvent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21267), [NSVA](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2208.04897)), which an Indian junior championship lacks. If league play-by-play exists for this event, the game clock read from the bug is what lines it up with the video. All of this depends on the stream showing a stable score bug, which is the first thing to check.

**The rim never moves in the world, and the pipeline should treat it that way.** Take the median of confident rim detections within each stretch where the camera holds still, and hold that box through dropouts. When the camera pans or zooms, move the box by the frame-to-frame camera motion estimated from background features with ORB plus RANSAC or with ECC. That is the same kind of affine warp BoT-SORT's camera-motion compensation already estimates ([BoT-SORT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.14651)). Reset the box at cuts. If the stream uses one or two fixed framings per hoop, a single click per framing plus a template or SAM2 track does the same job. The research notes estimate that this turns 4–57% rim coverage into near-continuous coverage without training; the 14 makes can confirm it. From this angle the rim is a thin sliver, so a larger "hoop" label covering rim and net is a better target.

Floor geometry cannot stand in for the rim. A court homography maps only points on the floor, and the rim sits **3.05 m** above it ([Dunkest](https://www.dunkest.com/en/nba/news/58065/rim-height-and-court-dimensions)). Neither open court-keypoint model documents rim or backboard keypoints ([koppolusameer](https://hf.co/koppolusameer/yolo11n-basketball-court-keypoints), [Roboflow](https://roboflow.com/blog/train-rf-detr-keypoint)). Projecting the rim would need a full camera model, which a low, grazing view makes unstable. A stable rim box also allows a cheap make cue that needs no ball: motion in the net region, measured after camera-motion compensation. It is untested. Roboflow's shot evaluator shows why such a cue matters: when the net hid the ball for about two frames, two real makes were recorded as misses ([Roboflow](https://roboflow.com/blog/how-to-build-an-ai-basketball-shot-evaluator)).

**The lasting fix is one to two days of labelling this venue's frames, mostly done by machines.** No one has published a zero-shot evaluation of Grounding DINO, OWLv2, YOLO-World, Florence-2 or SAM3 on basketballs or rims. Roboflow's own 2025 basketball pipeline fine-tunes RF-DETR rather than relying on a zero-shot detector ([Roboflow](https://roboflow.com/blog/identify-basketball-players)). Open-vocabulary models therefore belong inside a human-reviewed loop, speeding up labelling.

Sample 500–1,500 frames, weighted toward the windows before score changes. The 14 makes alone give 1,750–2,100 frames before keeping every second or third. Add random play and a few background frames. Pre-label the rim with Grounding DINO-tiny (172M parameters) or OWLv2-base (155M), both Apache-2.0 ([Grounding DINO](https://hf.co/IDEA-Research/grounding-dino-tiny), [OWLv2](https://hf.co/google/owlv2-base-patch16-ensemble)). Better still, click the rim once per camera shot and let SAM 2.1 carry it through the shot; its Apache-2.0 tiny model runs at **91.2 FPS on an A100** ([SAM2](https://github.com/facebookresearch/sam2)). For the ball, place one box at release and propagate it through the flight. Keep only tracks that last several frames along a smooth path. SAM2's example code runs in bfloat16, which the T4's Turing architecture does not support natively, so switch to fp16. YOLO-World is GPL-3.0 and so usable, but its V2.1-X scores 28.6 LVIS AP at 640 px ([YOLO-World](https://github.com/AILab-CVC/YOLO-World)). That trails OWLv2 on a third-party zero-shot leaderboard ([CodeSOTA](https://codesota.com/tasks/zero-shot-object-detection)), so it is better as a fast detector to train than as a labeller.

Review every frame by hand. CVAT's SAM2 tracker is an Enterprise or Online feature ([CVAT](https://docs.cvat.ai/docs/enterprise/segment-anything-2-tracker)). Run SAM2 in Colab and import its output into open-source CVAT instead, or use SAMannot, a local open-source SAM2 labelling tool ([SAMannot](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.11301)). autodistill automates the loop, but it runs "with no human intervention in between", and each plugin keeps its own licence ([autodistill](https://github.com/autodistill/autodistill)). Fine-tune YOLO11s from the NBA weights on mixed NBA and venue frames. Then collect the low-confidence frames and broken tracks near the rim for a second round. The closest evidence for the payoff is indirect. In Roboflow's pipeline, auto-labelling plus human correction lifted SmolVLM2's jersey reads from 56% to 86% ([Roboflow](https://roboflow.com/blog/identify-basketball-players)). The expected outcome is a rim found almost everywhere and much better ball recall near it, but no source measures this exact shift.

**Temporal models are the second step, not the first.** WASB is an MIT-licensed, 1.5M-parameter network that reads three stacked 288×512 frames and predicts a ball heatmap. It is the only small temporal ball model with basketball results, and its NBA camera "frequently moves and zooms in rapidly". It scores **82.6 F1 against 57.2** for the single-frame DeepBall-Large on that data ([WASB](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.05237v1)). There are three catches. Its weights come from NBA footage, the same shift that broke YOLO11s. Its training code is still "TBA" ([WASB-SBDT](https://github.com/nttcom/WASB-SBDT)). And the NBA data page returns 404, with no stated terms ([data page](https://ruiyan1995.github.io/SAM.html)). Testing the released weights zero-shot on the 14 make windows takes under an hour. Fine-tuning on point labels takes 2–4 days, mostly spent writing the missing training loop; mark the ball at the centre of its blur streak, as BlurBall recommends ([BlurBall](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.18387)). TrackNetV3 ships MIT training code and beat YOLOv7 by **98.56 to 68.00 F1** on shuttlecocks ([TrackNetV3](https://github.com/qaz812345/TrackNetV3)). But its background estimation, like TrackNetV4's frame differencing ([TrackNetV4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14543v1)), assumes a mostly static camera, so it fits only fixed half-court framings.

Learned event spotting comes last. T-DEED (GPL-3.0) beats E2E-Spot (BSD-3) when events must be located to within one frame: **71.48–73.23 against 68.40 mAP** on FineDiving. Both use RegNetY-200MF or 800MF backbones on 224-px frames, and each label is a single frame index ([T-DEED](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.05392v2), [E2E-Spot](https://github.com/jhong93/spot)). Trained on crops centred on the persistent rim box, with events seeded from score-bug timestamps, the spotter can learn to recognise the ball going through the net without detecting it. Neither model has a published basketball made/missed result or T4 figure, so treat it as a 3–5-day experiment. Heavier systems such as BasketEvent's PlayNet combine TimeSformer, SAM3 and Qwen2.5-VL to reach 0.682 macro F1, and they do not fit a free T4 ([BasketEvent](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.21267v1)).

## Online ReID cannot reach the re-entries behind 694 identities

The counts point to fragmentation, not swapping. About 28 identities per real person in football and 88 in basketball means most players get a new ID whenever they leave a panning frame and come back. The 441 kit-change splits are **38% of all basketball identities**, the clearest sign that the colour-flip split rule fires too often. Turning on `with_reid` cannot fix the main failure, for a reason visible in Ultralytics' source. BoT-SORT computes a box-overlap (IoU) distance and an appearance distance. It sets the appearance distance to 1.0 for every pair whose boxes do not overlap enough, then takes the smaller of the two distances (`np.minimum`) ([bot_sort.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ultralytics/ultralytics/main/ultralytics/trackers/bot_sort.py)). The docs define `proximity_thresh` as the "minimum IoU required for a valid ReID match" ([Ultralytics](https://docs.ultralytics.com/modes/track/)). Appearance can therefore only lower the cost of boxes that already overlap. It cannot block a swap that box overlap favours, and it cannot re-link a returning player whose box overlaps nothing.

Other trackers share the limit. Deep-EIoU, the base of GTA's best result, combines costs the same way ([Deep-EIoU](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hsiangwei0903/Deep-EIoU/main/Deep-EIoU/tracker/Deep_EIoU.py)). DeepSORT does use appearance as a hard gate, but its IoU fallback for one-frame-old tracks bypasses that gate ([DeepSORT](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1703.07402)). The published numbers agree: ReID moves BoT-SORT's HOTA by **+1.7 on SportsMOT and −0.2 on SoccerNet** ([roboflow/trackers](https://github.com/roboflow/trackers)). A longer track buffer is not the answer either; Ultralytics warns that raising it "increases ID switches risk" ([botsort.yaml](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ultralytics/ultralytics/main/ultralytics/cfg/trackers/botsort.yaml)). The fix has to happen offline, after tracking.

**Repair the kit-split rule before adding anything.** Per-frame Lab colour is noisy. Occlusion, court reflections in the torso crop and kits of similar brightness all corrupt it, so even a lasting flip is weak evidence of a new person. Many of the 441 splits are likely occlusion swaps or colour noise. Split only when a flip persists and coincides with a change of appearance-embedding cluster; GTA's DBSCAN splitter provides exactly that check. Then add the veto BoT-SORT lacks. Subclass `BOTSORT`, override `get_dists`, and call the parent. Set the cost to 1.0 wherever the raw embedding distance exceeds a veto threshold, or where track and detection carry different team labels and both labels are confident. That is 10–15 lines, and it stays AGPL-clean because Ultralytics is AGPL. It attacks cross-team swaps directly. A veto that fires on noisy colour labels would create new fragments, so restrict it to high-confidence labels. Where Ultralytics registers its trackers, and so how to plug in the subclass, was not verified.

**Offline stitching across the whole clip is the best-evidenced single fix for fragmentation.** GTA works in two passes. A splitter breaks up tracklets that contain more than one person, using DBSCAN over per-box OSNet appearance embeddings. A connector then merges tracklets step by step by mean cosine distance, never joining two that overlap in time, until no pair is closer than α=0.4 ([GTA](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.08216v1)). The closest published match to this pipeline's motion-only BoT-SORT is ByteTrack. On ByteTrack, GTA added **+6.28 HOTA and +12.40 IDF1 on SportsMOT**, and +4.67 HOTA and +9.45 IDF1 on SoccerNet (with ground-truth boxes), cutting ID switches by 31–33%. Detection accuracy barely moved, so the whole gain comes from linking. The connector alone supplies most of it: +6.05 of the +6.28 HOTA on SportsMOT ([GTA](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.08216v1)). The MIT code reads MOT-format text files through a two-step command-line tool ([gta-link](https://github.com/sjc042/gta-link)), so adding it takes about a day.

Four adaptations matter. First, GTA's spatial constraint assumes a fixed camera, so for panning broadcast disable it with a large `--spatial_factor`, or compute it in pitch coordinates once registration exists. Second, GTA was validated on clips averaging 485 frames ([SportsMOT](https://github.com/MCG-NJU/SportsMOT)), and 700–1,150 tracklets over 10–14 minutes give far more chances to merge teammates in the same kit. Tighten α toward 0.3 and add hard cannot-links: no merge across confidently different teams or confidently different jersey numbers, and a bonus when confident numbers match, which turns the pipeline's current (team, number) merge into a special case. Third, the default checkpoint, `sports_model.pth.tar-60`, is OSNet trained on SportsMOT ([gta-link](https://github.com/sjc042/gta-link)), whose non-commercial licence rules it out for shipping. Fourth, compute stays small. OSNet costs 0.27–0.57 GFLOPs per crop at its x0_5–x0_75 widths ([torchreid zoo](https://kaiyangzhou.github.io/deep-person-reid/MODEL_ZOO)), so embedding every second to fifth frame is minutes of T4 time, and comparing one mean embedding per tracklet keeps the roughly 1,000 × 1,000 connector matrix to seconds. Both figures are estimates, since GTA publishes no runtime. Whether long panning clips gain more than short ones (more fragments to join) or less (more chances for wrong merges) is unknown until the evaluation harness measures it.

**A licence-clean ReID model can come from the footage itself.** The only published case of large linking gains is ReID trained on sports footage that models body parts separately. PRTreID lifted StrongSORT by **+9.8 AssA and +9.3 IDF1** on SoccerNet, taking HOTA from 54.86 to 59.77 ([PRTreID](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2401.09942)). But it is trained on SoccerNet data, which sits behind an NDA ([SoccerNet](https://pypi.org/project/SoccerNet)). Weights trained on pedestrians mostly encode clothing colour, which separates teams but not teammates; SoccerNet's own ReID README notes that same-team players "have very similar appearance" ([sn-reid](https://github.com/SoccerNet/sn-reid)). The clean route starts from torchreid's MIT-licensed OSNet ([torchreid](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KaiyangZhou/deep-person-reid/master/LICENSE)), initialised from ImageNet, and fine-tunes it on each video's own tracklets. Crops from the same tracklet are positive pairs; crops from different tracklets in the same frame are negatives. No one has published this exact setup; the research notes estimate tens of minutes on a T4. The same embedding then serves GTA, the veto, team clustering and the jersey outlier filter.

A few alternatives deserve a look. BoxMOT (AGPL-3.0) deserves one run on the harness. Its new "occluboost" tracker leads SportsMOT-val at **83.17 HOTA against 76.93** for BoT-SORT, but the README names no detector, ReID weights or speed, and no paper describes the method ([BoxMOT](https://github.com/mikel-brostrom/boxmot)). StrongSORT's AFLink linker and GSI gap-filler are cheap, at 591.9 and 140.9 Hz on MOT17. But they are GPL-3.0, ignore appearance, and were trained on pedestrians ([StrongSORT](https://github.com/dyhBUPT/StrongSORT)). AFLink relies on continuity in image coordinates, which cannot bridge long off-screen gaps, and GSI fills missed detections rather than reducing identities. The SoccerNet game-state pipeline is GPL-3.0 and pinned to PyTorch 1.13 ([sn-gamestate](https://github.com/SoccerNet/sn-gamestate)). Borrow its ideas, not the stack: per-tracklet voting of role and jersey number, and team clustering on mean embeddings.

### Tracklet embeddings, not per-crop colour, should decide teams

Roboflow's `TeamClassifier` is a drop-in, licence-clean upgrade. The MIT code averages SigLIP-base image features (Apache-2.0), reduces them to three dimensions with UMAP, and splits them into two teams with KMeans. It is fitted once on player crops from every 60th frame ([team.py](https://github.com/roboflow/sports/blob/main/sports/common/team.py), [SigLIP](https://huggingface.co/google/siglip-base-patch16-224)). Its soccer example predicts a team per frame without voting per track ([main.py](https://github.com/roboflow/sports/blob/main/examples/soccer/main.py)), so keep the pipeline's majority vote. Predict on at most ten sampled crops per tracklet: 7,000–12,000 crops for 700–1,150 tracklets. That keeps a ViT-B-sized encoder to seconds or minutes on a T4, for about 20 lines of integration.

No study compares SigLIP with colour clustering on the same data, so the case rests on evidence from different datasets. Clustering CIELAB colour scored **91.5%** team accuracy in one soccer pipeline ([PlayerTV](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.16076v1)). KMeans on embeddings learned per video scored **95.17–95.6%** on SoccerNet ([PRTreID](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2401.09942)). Contrastive embeddings trained per game reached 94% after one frame and 97% within 500 frames on hockey ([Koshkina et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10068)). The datasets differ, so the gap only shows direction.

The bigger structural gain is clustering one mean embedding per tracklet instead of colours per crop. That removes the frame-to-frame noise behind the kit splits. The per-video OSNet can replace SigLIP here and then serve teams and identity at once. Keep referees out of the fit by using the detector's referee class, as Roboflow does. Nobody has evaluated three-way clustering for referees, and a third cluster is just as likely to capture one team's kit in shadow. Goalkeepers go to the nearer team centroid until registration lets them be assigned by the half they defend.

## One 1280-pixel detector cuts football GPU time about sixfold

The football stack runs two YOLOv8x models and a COCO YOLO11s at 1280 px on every frame. The arithmetic shows that no runtime trick can rescue this design on a T4. Ultralytics' TensorRT 10 latencies on a T4 at 640 px are **14.37 ms for YOLOv8x, 4.7 ms for YOLO11m and YOLO26m, and 2.5 ms for YOLO11s and YOLO26s** ([Ultralytics compare](https://docs.ultralytics.com/compare/yolov8-vs-yolo11/), [YOLO26](https://docs.ultralytics.com/models/yolo26/)). Scaled by pixel count to 1280, that is about 57 ms per YOLOv8x and 10 ms for YOLO11s. The stack thus needs roughly 125 ms of GPU time per frame, a ceiling near 8 fps even under TensorRT, before tracking and OCR. A single YOLO26m or YOLO11m at 1280 costs about 19 ms. These are linear extrapolations, and small models often scale better than linearly, but a sixfold gap survives that uncertainty.

Roboflow designed for a single model: its football "player" model already detects players, goalkeepers, referees and the ball ([roboflow/sports](https://github.com/roboflow/sports/tree/main/examples/soccer)). Its YOLOv8x baseline at 1280 is strong on players (mAP50 **0.928**) and weak only on the ball (mAP50 **0.507**, recall 0.404). Those figures come from a validation split of just 43 images ([training notebook](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/roboflow/sports/main/examples/soccer/notebooks/train_player_detector.ipynb)), and the weak ball class is why a second ball model exists. Train one four-class YOLO26m or YOLO11m at 1280 on the merged player and ball datasets. On COCO, YOLO26m gives up only 0.8 mAP50-95 points to YOLOv8x and YOLO11m gives up 2.4. YOLO26 also changes how training assigns labels to small targets such as the ball ([YOLO26](https://docs.ultralytics.com/models/yolo26/), [Ultralytics compare](https://docs.ultralytics.com/compare/yolov8-vs-yolo11/)). Ball recall on held-out frames from the user's own clips decides whether the swap goes ahead. Drop the COCO-person model only after checking the merged model's player recall.

Merging needs care, because Ultralytics warns that partial labelling breaks training ([Ultralytics](https://docs.ultralytics.com/yolov5/tutorials/tips_for_best_training_results/)). Any image labelled only for the ball needs its people labelled automatically by the current models first. That step also distils knowledge from the YOLOv8x models into the new one. This matters because Ultralytics' built-in distillation (`distill_model`, v8.4.77) accepts only teachers from the same model family, so YOLOv8x cannot teach a YOLO26 student directly. A YOLO26x teacher added 0.8 mAP to YOLO26m on COCO ([Ultralytics KD](https://docs.ultralytics.com/guides/knowledge-distillation)). Roboflow's YOLOv8x run took 0.677 h for 50 epochs at 1280 on an L4 ([training notebook](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/roboflow/sports/main/examples/soccer/notebooks/train_player_detector.ipynb)). No T4 figure exists, but a mid-size model at batch 4–8 should fit a free Colab session. Public copies of the training data list CC BY 4.0 ([Universe fork](https://universe.roboflow.com/kick-data/football-players-detection-3zvbc-7ocfe-jzrw7)), which an AGPL project can use with attribution. The original dataset page could not be read.

Export the model as a TensorRT FP16 engine with a fixed input size, built on the T4 itself. Across 19 Ampere-to-Hopper GPUs, TensorRT FP16 ran **1.85–2.55× faster than ONNX FP16** at the median, with accuracy within 0.001 mAP ([Ultralytics benchmark](https://www.ultralytics.com/blog/onnx-vs-tensorrt)). No Turing GPU was in that study, and no source compares PyTorch FP16 with TensorRT FP16 on a T4. Engines are "not a portable format" ([Ultralytics TensorRT](https://docs.ultralytics.com/integrations/tensorrt/)), so build one per GPU and TensorRT version, with a separate engine for the 4 GB laptop. Cache each on Drive under a name that records both, because Colab images change. A fixed input size avoids the slowdowns from variable shapes and size mismatches that the same page warns about. Exporting the current three models to FP16 engines takes minutes and is a fair stopgap, but the 8 fps ceiling remains.

Once detection falls to 10–20 ms, the free tier's two vCPUs become the bottleneck ([Paperspace](https://blog.paperspace.com/alternative-to-google-colab-pro/)). Move PARSeq from CPU onnxruntime to the GPU (the CUDA execution provider) with OnnxTR's `[gpu]` extra. OnnxTR is Apache-2.0 and also loads custom PARSeq ONNX files ([OnnxTR](https://github.com/felixdittrich92/OnnxTR)). Batch all number crops in a frame, and read each track only every 3–5 frames when the locator fires, as Roboflow's every-5-frames sampling does ([Roboflow](https://roboflow.com/blog/identify-basketball-players)). Reference PARSeq takes a **14.87 ms median per crop** on one thread ([PARSeq](https://github.com/baudm/parseq)), so a dozen visible players cost about as much as the new detector.

Several other levers help. Keep sparse-optical-flow GMC for pans: turning it off saves only "a few ms/frame" and suits static cameras ([Ultralytics](https://docs.ultralytics.com/modes/track/)). Run decoding, inference and drawing in separate threads. Decoding on the GPU also frees the CPU. A T4 has two hardware video decoders (NVDEC) ([NVIDIA](https://docs.nvidia.com/video-technologies/pynvvideocodec/pynvc-api-prog-guide/application-note.html)). TorchCodec's BSD-3 decoder with `device="cuda"` ([TorchCodec](https://github.com/meta-pytorch/torchcodec)) avoids PyNvVideoCodec, whose MIT listing on PyPI conflicts with an NVIDIA SDK licence in its docs ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/PyNvVideoCodec)). Detecting every second frame and letting the tracker predict in between halves detector cost, but it hurts the small, fast ball most.

### Fine-tuned digits add seven points; the vote already exists

Koshkina and Elder's ablations make the jersey path unusually well evidenced. Inside their pipeline, off-the-shelf PARSeq reads **80.51%** of SoccerNet test tracklets correctly. A PARSeq fine-tuned on hockey reads 83.90%, and one fine-tuned on soccer **87.45%**. But the soccer model drops to 65.84% on hockey ([Koshkina & Elder](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.13896)). Fine-tuned recognisers are therefore domain-specific, so football and basketball need separate models or one trained on both. The pipeline's per-track vote with a two-digit bias already mirrors their rule-based consolidation; the bias alone is worth 0.66 points there. What the pipeline lacks is their main-subject filter. It drops crops whose appearance embedding looks unlike the rest of the track, which removes occluding players, and it is worth **2.89 points**. The per-video OSNet or Ultralytics' detector features can supply that embedding. A legibility gate is the third piece. Their ResNet34 gate reached 94.5% accuracy and 71.7% F1 on hockey ([Koshkina & Elder](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.13896)), but confidence thresholds on the locator and recogniser make a cheap first version.

Fine-tune Apache-2.0 PARSeq with a digits-only character set and `max_label_length=2`, as they did for 25 epochs ([PARSeq](https://github.com/baudm/parseq)). Then export it to ONNX for OnnxTR. At 23.8M parameters and 32×128 input, training on a few thousand crops should take minutes to under an hour on a T4, though no one reports a figure. This path replaces the docTR weights in use today.

Where the labels come from is the licence question. Koshkina's code and weights are CC BY-NC 3.0 ([repo](https://github.com/mkoshkina/jersey-number-pipeline)). SoccerNet's jersey set states no licence ([sn-jersey](https://github.com/SoccerNet/sn-jersey)), and its source broadcasts sit behind an NDA ([SoccerNet](https://www.soccer-net.org/data)). Weights trained on either are for research only. The clean source is the pipeline's own output. A track with a confident vote gives a label that can be copied to its legible frames; Koshkina used the same copying for SoccerNet's per-tracklet labels. Then review the result by hand.

For basketball, Roboflow's fine-tuned ResNet digit classifier reached **93%**, against 86% for fine-tuned SmolVLM2 and 56% for SmolVLM2 zero-shot ([Roboflow](https://roboflow.com/blog/identify-basketball-players)). But a classifier over a fixed set of numbers cannot output one it never saw in training, and Roboflow's data held 315 crops of "8" against 8 of "6". A digits-only PARSeq handles unseen numbers. The best published results are higher still: 92.85% for the 2023 SoccerNet winner's ensemble of text recognisers and 90.95% for the runner-up's fine-tuned CLIP ([SoccerNet 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.06006)). Both carry research-scale costs. Torso crops from pose keypoints matter less here, because the YOLO11n locator already finds the digits. As a fallback, AGPL YOLO26n-pose runs in 1.8 ms on a T4 ([Ultralytics pose](https://docs.ultralytics.com/tasks/pose/)).

## Registration and cut detection turn pixels into evidence

A homography that passes quality checks is the piece several other fixes depend on. It lets GTA's spatial check ignore pans. It assigns goalkeepers by the half they defend rather than by image position. And it removes detections in dugouts and the crowd, whose share of the identity counts nobody has measured.

For football, the quickest licence-clean option is Roboflow's `football-pitch-detection.pt`, a 32-keypoint YOLOv8x-pose model. Its weights are AGPL-3.0 and its `sports` code is MIT ([soccer README](https://github.com/roboflow/sports/blob/main/examples/soccer/README.md)). Its `main` branch is not ready to ship. `ViewTransformer` calls `cv2.findHomography` with no method flag ([view.py](https://github.com/roboflow/sports/blob/main/sports/common/view.py)), which OpenCV treats as plain least squares ([OpenCV](https://docs.opencv.org/4.x/d9/d0c/group__calib3d.html#ga4abc2ece9fab9398f2e560d53c8c9780)). One misplaced keypoint therefore skews the whole map, and the roadmap still lists smoothing "to eliminate flickering" as undone. An unmerged branch adds RANSAC at 10 px, a check on mean reprojection error, and a minimum of four points ([homography.py](https://github.com/roboflow/sports/blob/feat/soccer-pr5-combo/sports/common/homography.py)).

Four more guards are needed. Vertices 14–17 of the template all lie on the halfway line ([soccer.py](https://github.com/roboflow/sports/blob/main/sports/configs/soccer.py)), so a midfield view whose only confident points are those four gives a **degenerate fit**; reject point sets that are collinear or cover too small an area. Smooth by projecting the template points through each accepted homography, applying a moving average to those points and refitting, rather than averaging matrix entries. On frames that fail the checks, reuse the last good homography, moved forward by the GMC motion estimate, for a second or two, and reset everything at cuts. Finally, set the template's 120 × 70 m to the real pitch, because on a 105 m pitch lengths otherwise read up to 14% long. This is 0.5–1.5 days of work. The model runs at an estimated 15–40 ms per frame in PyTorch FP16 on a T4, and less if run every few frames. It was trained on frames from the DFL Bundesliga Data Shootout ([soccer README](https://github.com/roboflow/sports/blob/main/examples/soccer/README.md)), whose Kaggle terms were not checked. Treat the weights as non-commercial until they are.

PnLCalib is the accuracy upgrade, and the licence-clean member of the SoccerNet calibration family. Its code is GPL-2.0-or-later, which its README says makes it "compatible with GPLv3 and AGPLv3 projects". Its weights are CC BY 4.0, commercial use allowed ([PnLCalib](https://github.com/mguti97/PnLCalib)). It reaches a SoccerNet 2022 final score of **79.5 against TVCalib's 54.8**, and a 0.60 m mean projection error on the WC14 benchmark. But it costs **164–439 ms per frame** on an RTX 2080 Ti plus a Xeon CPU ([PnLCalib paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.08401v5)). On Colab's two vCPUs it belongs on keyframes, or as an offline teacher that labels frames for fine-tuning the YOLO pitch model on this camera. Its predecessor NBJW ships a GPL-2.0 licence without "or later" wording ([NBJW](https://github.com/mguti97/No-Bells-Just-Whistles/blob/main/LICENSE)). FSF guidance treats GPL-2.0-only as incompatible with the GPLv3 family ([FSF](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/license-list.html)), so skip it. TVCalib is MIT but less accurate and slower, because it optimises iteratively ([TVCalib](https://github.com/MM4SPA/tvcalib)). If simple smoothing falls short, BHITK smooths homographies over time with a two-stage Kalman filter ([BHITK](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.10361)).

Basketball needs its own model and template. Every open court-keypoint model found comes from Roboflow's `basketball-court-detection-2` data, which centres on NBA courts. None reports accuracy on FIBA or amateur courts. The AGPL koppolusameer YOLO11n has no map from its keypoints to court positions, and the AnzeZ YOLO11m-pose has no licence ([koppolusameer](https://huggingface.co/koppolusameer/yolo11n-basketball-court-keypoints), [AnzeZ](https://huggingface.co/AnzeZ/basketball-court-yolo11m-pose)). Train a YOLO11s/m-pose on that data plus 100–300 labelled frames from this venue, with `fliplr=0` and `mosaic=0`. AnzeZ's run reached **0.947 pose mAP50-95** in 2,296 s over 80 epochs on an unnamed GPU ([results.csv](https://huggingface.co/AnzeZ/basketball-court-yolo11m-pose/blob/main/results.csv)). Roboflow says a Colab T4 is enough for its RF-DETR keypoint alternative ([Roboflow](https://roboflow.com/blog/train-rf-detr-keypoint)). Map the keypoints with the `CourtConfiguration` on `sports@feat/basketball`, which has a FIBA preset. The FIBA three-point arc is **6.75 m against the NBA's 7.24 m** ([config.py](https://github.com/roboflow/sports/blob/feat/basketball/sports/basketball/config.py)), so an NBA template corrupts the fit on this court. From a low stands camera the crowd hides the far sideline, so accept a homography only when keypoints span both near and far court. A bachelor's thesis on amateur footage found that poor line visibility, camera motion and domain shift "reduced homography stability" ([Theseus](https://www.theseus.fi/handle/10024/909540)).

With a checked homography, several features become nearly free. Goalkeepers join the team whose outfield players' median pitch-x lies in the keeper's half, a mapping that changes only at half-time. Detections whose feet project more than about 3 m beyond the touchlines, or about 1 m beyond the court, can be dropped once the homography passes its checks, with the margins tuned per venue. Two-versus-three-point labels follow from the shooter's feet at release against the arc radius, with shots within about 0.5 m of the line flagged as uncertain. Speed and distance need the most care. Even PnLCalib leaves a 0.60 m mean error on WC14 ([PnLCalib paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.08401v5)), and at 25 fps, 0.3–0.6 m of frame-to-frame jitter reads as **7–15 m/s of false speed**. Smooth positions over 0.3–0.5 s before computing speed, and never sum distance across cuts.

### Cut detection is an hour's work that protects every stage

Detecting shot boundaries is a solved problem with permissively licensed tools. PySceneDetect (BSD-3, CPU-only) reaches **91.59 F1 on BBC** with `AdaptiveDetector`, scored at exact-frame tolerance. Its parameter sweep found `adaptive_threshold=3.5, window_width=3, min_scene_len=0.6s` best overall ([PySceneDetect benchmark](https://github.com/Breakthrough/PySceneDetect/blob/main/benchmark/README.md)). Its weak spot is gradual transitions: on ClipShots fades, its best detector manages 75.33 F1 ([PySceneDetect benchmark](https://github.com/Breakthrough/PySceneDetect/blob/main/benchmark/README.md)). TransNetV2 (MIT) handles those, scoring 96.2 F1 on BBC and 77.9 on ClipShots from 48×27-pixel frames. Its official inference pins TensorFlow 2.1, so budget half a day for the PyTorch port with converted weights ([TransNetV2](https://github.com/soCzech/TransNetV2)). The two benchmarks score differently, so their BBC numbers are not directly comparable.

Replays are harder, since no maintained open replay detector exists for basketball. The cheapest robust signal is a check that the frame is a wide view that can be registered: at least four non-collinear keypoints and an acceptable reprojection error. The same check catches close-ups, where registration methods fail for lack of landmarks ([PnLCalib paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.08401v5)). Broadcasters start and end replays with logo animations of roughly 10–30 frames ([Tong et al.](https://elcvia.cvc.uab.cat/article/download/v7-n1-tong-liu-u/171)). A few hand-cropped templates of this broadcaster's logo catch most of the rest, and the score bug disappearing is another cue to check on this footage.

When the video cuts to a view that cannot be registered, pause tracking without creating new identities. If a wide view returns within a few seconds, re-link players in pitch or court coordinates by team and appearance. Otherwise reset the tracker, the homography smoothing, the GMC state and the shot state machine. A deployed badminton broadcast system removed replays before tracking for the same reason ([arXiv 2308.12199](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12199)).

## Licence risk lives in weights and datasets, not code

Code compatibility is the easy part. GPLv3 section 13 explicitly allows combining GPLv3 and AGPLv3 works ([GNU GPLv3](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/gpl-3.0.html)), and the FSF lists Apache-2.0 as compatible with GPLv3 ([FSF](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/license-list.html#apache2)). MIT, BSD, Apache-2.0, GPL-3.0 and GPL-2.0-or-later code can therefore all sit inside an AGPL-3.0 project, and every code component on the recommended path falls in that set. TrackEval, torchreid, gta-link, WASB, TrackNetV3, TransNetV2 and roboflow/sports are MIT. PARSeq, OnnxTR, SigLIP, Grounding DINO, OWLv2, SAM 2.1, RF-DETR and roboflow/trackers are Apache-2.0. PySceneDetect, TorchCodec and E2E-Spot are BSD-3; T-DEED, YOLO-World, StrongSORT and sn-gamestate are GPL-3.0; PnLCalib is GPL-2.0-or-later; and Ultralytics and BoxMOT are AGPL-3.0. ## Conclusion

The six weaknesses look independent, but each fix feeds the next, and the ranking follows that chain. Three signals the broadcast already carries feed nearly every later stage: the score bug, the fixed rim and the cuts. So does one set of constraints the pipeline already computes: team and jersey labels. Score-bug events pick the frames the venue fine-tune needs, then grade the shot detector it produces. Registration lets stitching ignore pans and settles goalkeeper sides. Confident jersey reads become cannot-links, and longer tracks give each vote more evidence. Better votes then tighten the next round of links. Building the signals before the models means every later model starts with better labels and a way to measure itself.

The more important finding is that, on every weak point of this pipeline, the accuracy fix and the licence fix are the same move. Train on the project's own footage, with labels the broadcast and the pipeline generate. Three sources supply them: venue frames picked by score changes, jersey labels from confident track votes, and an OSNet fitted to each video's tracklets. They close the domain gap that NBA, SoccerNet and SportsMOT weights leave open, and they replace exactly the non-commercial and NDA-restricted weights that rule the strongest published components out of shipping. That makes a self-labelling loop, not any single model, the project's most lasting asset. No public benchmark can replace it, because the research found no permissively licensed dataset from a low stands camera at an amateur venue like this one.
