# Track-level voting beats heavier models on T4

On a free Colab T4, the most defensible 2024–2026 open-source design for broadcast football and basketball is one light detector feeding **Ultralytics BoT-SORT with sparse-optical-flow camera motion compensation (CMC)**. Every downstream label (team, jersey number, shot outcome, ball position) is then decided by consensus across a track's frames, not by a heavier per-frame model. Sports tracking fails on association rather than detection. In like-for-like harnesses BoT-SORT **beats ByteTrack by 0.8 to 9 HOTA on SportsMOT** ([Roboflow trackers](https://trackers.roboflow.com/latest/evaluations/results/), [BoxMOT](https://github.com/mikel-brostrom/boxmot)). Deep OC-SORT and StrongSORT score about 3 HOTA higher still in BoxMOT's harness, but each has a catch: Ultralytics ships Deep OC-SORT with CMC off, and StrongSORT needs BoxMOT as an extra dependency ([BoxMOT](https://github.com/mikel-brostrom/boxmot), [Ultralytics](https://docs.ultralytics.com/modes/track/)). Offline tracklet stitching keeps IDs better than online ReID does: GTA's offline pass **added 3.7 HOTA on SoccerNet** ([GTA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.08216)), while ReID added nothing there in Roboflow's harness. Teams come from the packaged SigLIP + UMAP + KMeans(2) recipe, with a CIELAB torso-color fallback that scored **91.5%** in a published soccer pipeline ([PlayerTV](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.16076v1)). Jersey numbers come from PARSeq on gated torso crops, with a confidence-thresholded vote per track. Raw EasyOCR (**11.3%**), raw PaddleOCR (**30.6%**) and zero-shot SmolVLM2 (**56%**) all trail fine-tuned small recognizers ([PlayerTV](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.16076v1), [Roboflow](https://roboflow.com/blog/identify-basketball-players)). For basketball shots, fuse Roboflow's 1.7-second action-class state machine with rim-relative ball geometry. For the ball, use a dedicated high-resolution detector that slices only on misses, selects candidates by motion gating and interpolates short gaps. The README should state the evidence gaps plainly. No source publishes T4 FPS for these trackers or OCR pipelines, nobody has compared SigLIP and color clustering head-to-head, and no open shot detector reports broadcast precision or recall. The same tracker's HOTA also moves about 10 points between harnesses, so the project must publish its own measurements.

## BoT-SORT's camera compensation beats ByteTrack by 0.8–9 HOTA

Sports tracking breaks on association, not detection. HOTA, the headline metric, combines detection accuracy (DetA) and association accuracy (AssA). On SportsMOT (240 basketball, volleyball and football videos), ByteTrack's **DetA is 77.1 but its AssA only 51.2**. Fast, variable-speed player motion violates the Kalman filter's linear assumption, and players look "similar yet distinguishable" ([SportsMOT](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.05170v2)). A broadcast pan makes this worse because it shifts every box at once. The constant-velocity prediction lands away from the new detection, and IoU matching fails. CMC estimates a global affine warp each frame and applies it to the Kalman states before matching. Ultralytics exposes it as `gmc_method`. The default is `sparseOptFlow`; `orb`, `sift`, `ecc` and `none` are the alternatives, and `ecc` "is more accurate but slower" ([Ultralytics Track docs](https://docs.ultralytics.com/modes/track/)). Roboflow's tracker evaluation recommends BoT-SORT "for sports broadcasts and other footage with frequent camera movement" ([Roboflow trackers](https://trackers.roboflow.com/latest/evaluations/results/)).

Absolute scores are only comparable within one harness. ByteTrack on SportsMOT scores **62.8–64.1 HOTA** in the dataset paper, **73.0** in Roboflow's harness and **67.93** in BoxMOT's validation run, because detectors and splits differ ([SportsMOT](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.05170v2), [Roboflow trackers](https://trackers.roboflow.com/latest/evaluations/results/), [BoxMOT](https://github.com/mikel-brostrom/boxmot)). The ordering within each harness is stable: BoT-SORT beats ByteTrack, and appearance-aware trackers beat both. BoxMOT puts StrongSORT (**79.80**) and Deep OC-SORT (**79.51**) about 3 HOTA above BoT-SORT (**76.93**). BoxMOT doesn't name its detector, though, and its 97–98 MOTA suggests near-oracle boxes. OC-SORT is the unstable one. It beats ByteTrack by about 9 HOTA in the SportsMOT paper, yet it ranks last in Roboflow's SoccerNet run (78.4, below even SORT's 81.6).

Integration cost settles the default. Ultralytics 8.4.63 ships six trackers: BoT-SORT, ByteTrack, OC-SORT, Deep OC-SORT, FastTracker and TrackTrack. Only BoT-SORT, Deep OC-SORT and TrackTrack support CMC, and Deep OC-SORT sets it to `none` by default. **TrackTrack is now the default when no `tracker=` argument is given** ([Ultralytics Track docs](https://docs.ultralytics.com/modes/track/)). None of the sources benchmark TrackTrack on sports, so pin the Ultralytics version and pass `tracker="botsort.yaml"` explicitly. StrongSORT is not in Ultralytics. It comes through BoxMOT, which requires `ultralytics>=8.4.58` ([BoxMOT](https://github.com/mikel-brostrom/boxmot)). The detector sets throughput. Given detections, OC-SORT association runs at "700+ FPS on a single CPU" ([OC-SORT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14360)). BoT-SORT's `model: auto` ReID reuses YOLO detector features, "adding minimal overhead" ([Ultralytics Track docs](https://docs.ultralytics.com/modes/track/)). No source reports T4 FPS for any tracker here. The only consumer-GPU figure is the SoccerNet 2024 game-state winner's raw tracking: up to 80 FPS on an RTX 3080 Ti laptop GPU, which needed FP16 TensorRT ([arXiv 2504.06357](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.06357v1)).

### Offline stitching adds 3.7 HOTA where online ReID adds none

Three levers keep an ID through occlusions and re-entries, in increasing cost. The first is a longer `track_buffer`, counted in frames. The default of 30 is about 1.2 s at 25 fps, and the config warns that raising it "increases ID switches risk" ([botsort.yaml](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ultralytics/ultralytics/main/ultralytics/cfg/trackers/botsort.yaml)). The second is online ReID. Setting `with_reid: True` with `model: auto` reuses detector features, and Ultralytics advises raising `appearance_thresh` to 0.85 or more for look-alike crowds ([Ultralytics Track docs](https://docs.ultralytics.com/modes/track/)). In sports the payoff is modest: **+1.7 HOTA on SportsMOT and −0.2 on SoccerNet** at default settings. Roboflow notes that ReID gains are smallest when targets look alike, which describes teammates in one kit ([Roboflow trackers](https://trackers.roboflow.com/latest/evaluations/results/)). The third is offline global association. GTA is a plug-in for any tracker: it splits tracklets that hold several identities and merges tracklets that belong to one. It lifts SoccerNet from **79.41 to 83.11 HOTA** and reaches **81.04 on SportsMOT** ([GTA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.08216)). StrongSORT's AFLink linking (**1.7 ms per image**) and GSI interpolation (**7.1 ms per image**) are lighter bolt-ons of the same kind ([StrongSORT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.13514)). Deep-EIoU, an online sports tracker, replaces the Kalman filter with expansion IoU and deep features. It scores 77.2 HOTA on SportsMOT and 85.4 on SoccerNet, but it is a separate codebase to integrate ([Deep-EIoU](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13074)). Mask propagation doesn't earn its cost on a T4. McByte needs SAM/Cutie dependencies to reach 76.9 HOTA on SportsMOT, within a point of tuned BoT-SORT + ReID at 77.8 ([Roboflow trackers](https://trackers.roboflow.com/latest/evaluations/results/)). Roboflow's SAM2 basketball pipeline runs at 1–2 FPS on a T4 ([Roboflow basketball](https://roboflow.com/blog/identify-basketball-players)).

Each lever fails differently. A long buffer combined with CMC drift lets a nearby player inherit the ID after a long pan. ReID swaps teammates whose embeddings nearly match, and zoom changes the crop scale. Offline stitching needs the whole clip, so it can't run live, and it propagates any bad split or merge. A global affine warp assumes a mostly static background, which hard cuts and replays break. Roboflow names C-BIoU as the fallback when CMC is "unavailable or harmful" ([Roboflow trackers](https://trackers.roboflow.com/latest/evaluations/results/)). Resetting the tracker at detected shot cuts is a cheap guard, though that is a design suggestion, not a measured result. Likewise, never carry `persist=True` state across unrelated videos ([Ultralytics Track docs](https://docs.ultralytics.com/modes/track/)). The practical recipe is BoT-SORT with CMC and a 60–150-frame buffer online. An offline merge pass then joins two tracklets only when their team labels match, their jersey reads are compatible, and the gap between them in time and space is plausible.

## SigLIP clustering is the default; CIELAB color scores 91.5% almost free

The de-facto open-source recipe is Roboflow's `TeamClassifier`. It embeds crops with `google/siglip-base-patch16-224`, mean-pools the token embeddings, reduces them to three dimensions with UMAP, and splits them with KMeans(2) ([team.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/roboflow/sports/main/sports/common/team.py)). Two details bite on Colab. The class expects ready-made crops, and its device defaults to `'cpu'`, so pass `device='cuda'` yourself. The soccer example fits once per video, on player crops from every 60th frame, detected at `imgsz=1280`. It fits only on the player class. Each goalkeeper then joins the team whose centroid is nearer. Referees have their own detector class and are never clustered ([main.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/roboflow/sports/main/examples/soccer/main.py)). Roboflow's basketball pipeline reuses the recipe, sampling one frame per second and cropping the central part of each box ([Roboflow basketball](https://roboflow.com/blog/identify-basketball-players)).

Color clustering is the cheap baseline, and the only one with a published accuracy. PlayerTV trims a torso region to avoid grass, takes its mean color, and runs KMeans with k=2. It scored **91.5% team accuracy in CIELAB versus 87.6% in weighted RGB**, and on one hard-lighting video weighted RGB fell to 76.2% ([PlayerTV](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.16076v1)). Learned embeddings score higher but need training. A contrastive network trained per video on k-means pseudo-labels reached **94% after one frame and 97% within 500 frames** on hockey ([Koshkina et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10068)). The SoccerNet 2024 game-state winner trained OSNet on 111 kit classes and clustered athletes into three groups: the two largest became the teams and the smallest the referees. It then found goalkeepers by clustering inside the penalty areas ([arXiv 2504.06357](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.06357v1)). It won with 63.81 GS-HOTA against a 23.36 baseline ([arXiv 2409.10587](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.10587)), but it reported no standalone team accuracy.

No source compares SigLIP and color clustering on the same footage, and Roboflow publishes no team accuracy. The default therefore rests on robustness arguments, not measurement. SigLIP takes the slot because it is packaged and needs no prior on kit colors. It also encodes pattern, sleeves and shorts as well as mean color. That should help where a single color axis collapses, as with similar kits or a pitch split into sunlit and shaded halves. Keep CIELAB as the fallback for CPU or low-VRAM runs. Two rules matter more than the choice of embedding. First, exclude goalkeepers and referees before fitting, or a distinctive kit can take over one of the two clusters. If the detector lacks those classes, use k=3 and treat the smallest cluster as referees. Second, smooth labels with a majority vote per track. No source measures that vote's gain, but the contrastive study points the same way: its accuracy rose from 94% to 97% as frames accumulated.

## Jersey accuracy hinges on crop gating and voting, not the OCR engine

Broadcast jerseys defeat generic OCR. The SoccerNet jersey benchmark has 2,853 tracklets plus a 1,211-tracklet challenge set. Each tracklet is labeled 1–99, or −1 for "not visible", and scoring is tracklet accuracy. Low resolution and motion blur make it hard, and a number may be legible in only a few frames ([SoccerNet 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.06006), [sn-jersey](https://github.com/SoccerNet/sn-jersey)). Run raw on soccer tracklets, **PaddleOCR reads 30.6% top-1 and EasyOCR 11.3%**. OCR accuracy fell to 0% on a poor-lighting video, and greedy EasyOCR over every crop of every frame ran at about 1 FPS on a V100 ([PlayerTV](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.16076v1)).

A similar recognizer does far better once the pipeline chooses its evidence. Koshkina and Elder's pipeline has five stages. It filters outlier frames with ReID features and drops illegible crops with a ResNet34 legibility classifier. It crops the torso from ViTPose shoulder and hip keypoints, reads the number with PARSeq, and consolidates the reads per tracklet. That reaches **87.45% on SoccerNet test and 79.31% on the challenge set** ([Koshkina & Elder 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.13896v1)). Their ablations show where the accuracy comes from. **Off-the-shelf PARSeq already scores 80.51%** inside the pipeline, and fine-tuning on soccer adds about 7 points. Removing the outlier filter costs 2.89 points, the confidence threshold 2.07, and the two-digit bias 0.66. PlayerTV used different data, so the gap from raw PaddleOCR is only directional. It still says crop selection and aggregation matter as much as the recognizer. Don't adopt their code. It needs four conda environments on PyTorch 1.9, keeps its weights on Google Drive and OneDrive, and is licensed CC BY-NC 3.0 ([repo](https://github.com/mkoshkina/jersey-number-pipeline)). PARSeq on its own is easy to adopt. It has **23.8M parameters, 3.26 GFLOPs and a 14.87 ms median latency** (hardware unstated). It is Apache-2.0 and loads from `torch.hub`, with a `parseq_tiny` variant ([baudm/parseq](https://github.com/baudm/parseq)).

Higher accuracy exists, but it costs ensembles or labels. The 2023 winner (**92.85%**) filtered its training images with DBNet++ and voted across five STR models (SVTR-tiny, SVTR-small, SATRN, NRTR, ASTER). The runner-up (**90.95%**) fine-tuned two CLIP models on pseudo-labels from zero-shot CLIP ViT-L/14, then took a tracklet majority over frames with probability above 0.7 ([SoccerNet 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.06006)). A detector-free single-stage ViT reaches **83.5–85.6% on the challenge set**, with public code ([Grad, CVPRW 2025](https://lukaszgrad.github.io/jnr/)). The 2024 game-state winner reads two digits from **32×32 crops** with a two-head ResNet-18 ([arXiv 2504.06357](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.06357v1)). Small VLMs are the weakest option per unit of compute. On 3.6k NBA number crops, SmolVLM2 scored **56% zero-shot and 86% fine-tuned, against 93% for a ResNet-32 classifier** ([Roboflow basketball](https://roboflow.com/blog/identify-basketball-players)). A quantized, fine-tuned SmolVLM2 still took about 23 s on a handful of frames ([Roboflow SmolVLM2](https://roboflow.com/blog/base-vs-fine-tuned-smolvlm2-ocr)), against roughly 15 ms per crop for PARSeq. No source reports jersey accuracy for Florence-2 or Qwen2-VL/Qwen2.5-VL. Don't expect the NBA figures to transfer to soccer wide shots, where numbers cover far fewer pixels.

The voting rules decide whether a T4 pipeline is accurate. Roboflow reads numbers every 5 frames and confirms one only after **3 consecutive identical reads**. It assigns a number box to a player's SAM2 mask when their intersection-over-smaller-area is at least 0.9 ([Roboflow basketball](https://roboflow.com/blog/identify-basketball-players)). The CLIP runner-up votes only over frames above 0.7 probability ([SoccerNet 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.06006)), and Koshkina adds a two-digit bias ([Koshkina & Elder 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.13896v1)). Combined, the recipe runs as follows. Skip boxes too short to be legible: start near 60–80 px tall and tune per video, because no source validates a threshold. Crop the torso from pose keypoints, or without pose take roughly the top 15–55% of the box. Run PARSeq every few frames and keep only one- or two-digit reads above a confidence threshold. Prefer two-digit strings, require at least three agreeing reads, and otherwise emit "unknown". Abstaining is correct behavior under the benchmark. Because −1 is a scored class, "unknown" on a tracklet whose number never shows counts as right.

## A 1.7-second state machine survives pans that break trajectory rules

The best-known rule-based repo, avishah3's AI-Basketball-Shot-Detection-Tracker, runs a two-class YOLOv8 (ball, hoop). It counts an attempt when the ball passes through an "up" zone and then a "down" zone below the hoop. The up zone spans ±4 hoop widths horizontally and 0.5–2 hoop heights above the hoop center. It scores a make by fitting a line through just two ball points around rim height. The make counts if that line crosses the rim within ±0.4 hoop widths plus a fixed **10 px** ([utils.py](https://github.com/avishah3/AI-Basketball-Shot-Detection-Tracker/blob/master/utils.py)). The README claims **95% make and 97% shot accuracy** without describing a test protocol, and says testing used outdoor phone video shot from the ground. It also states that the tool "will not work if multiple basketballs and hoops are in frame" ([README](https://github.com/avishah3/AI-Basketball-Shot-Detection-Tracker)). The repo has no license ([GitHub API](https://api.github.com/repos/avishah3/AI-Basketball-Shot-Detection-Tracker)). On broadcast, every zone is in absolute pixels, anchored to the last accepted hoop box. A filter also rejects hoop moves larger than half the box diagonal within 5 frames ([utils.py](https://github.com/avishah3/AI-Basketball-Shot-Detection-Tracker/blob/master/utils.py)). A fast pan therefore leaves the anchor behind and mixes camera motion into the ball's arc. On a wide shot the fixed 10 px is a large fraction of a small rim, which inflates false makes. Expressing ball positions relative to each frame's rim box, normalized by rim width, fixes both problems.

Roboflow's make-or-miss notebook avoids ball trajectories entirely. RF-DETR-S (Apache-2.0) labels each frame. It is trained on the 10-class `basketball-player-detection-3` dataset, whose classes include ball, ball-in-basket, rim, player-jump-shot and player-layup-dunk ([Roboflow basketball](https://roboflow.com/blog/identify-basketball-players)). A state machine starts a shot after **3 consecutive jump-shot or layup-dunk frames**. It calls the shot MADE after **2 consecutive ball-in-basket frames within 1.7 s**, and MISSED at that deadline. It requires 0.5 s between starts and a 0.5 s cooldown after a make ([tools.py](https://github.com/roboflow/sports/blob/feat/basketball/sports/basketball/tools.py), [notebook](https://github.com/roboflow/notebooks/blob/main/notebooks/basketball-ai-make-or-miss-jumpshot-detection.ipynb)). Because each frame is judged alone, pans matter only when they hurt detection. The ball-in-basket class also sidesteps the 2D ambiguity of a ball passing in front of the rim. The demo clips are NBA broadcast footage. The notebook, though, publishes no precision or recall, targets a Colab L4 rather than a T4, and loads Roboflow-hosted weights with an API key ([notebook](https://github.com/roboflow/notebooks/blob/main/notebooks/basketball-ai-make-or-miss-jumpshot-detection.ipynb)). Its design has two failure modes. A tip-in or putback that never looks like a jump shot or layup never starts a shot, so it silently drops out of the attempt count. Two false ball-in-basket frames produce a false MADE, and the detector's `rim` class is never used to check for either case.

Roboflow's 2026 shot evaluator shows why 2D geometry stays ambiguous. A net that hid the ball for about two frames turned **two real makes into misses**. The author also notes that "certain front-rim bounce-outs look identical to rattle-in makes from one pov". The evaluator's fix is physical. It sets pixel scale from the rim's 18-inch (0.4572 m) inner diameter and detects release when the ball moves 0.35 m from the wrist. It then classifies a reappearing ball by its fall speed. Between 10% and 90% of free-fall speed is a make, about 100% is an airball behind the rim, and about 0% is a loose ball ([shot evaluator](https://roboflow.com/blog/how-to-build-an-ai-basketball-shot-evaluator)). Learned models are no shortcut. R(2+1)D-18 reaches **69.3%** 8-way accuracy on Basketball-51 broadcast clips but drops to 43.7% when the center of the frame is blurred, a sign of shortcut learning ([arXiv 2610.33263](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.33263)). PL-NBA's 3-point made and missed F1 scores are only **0.71 and 0.74**, and its data is CC BY-NC 4.0 ([PL-NBA](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.19646v1)). BasketEvent's best overall F1 is 0.682, its data is unreleased, and its pipeline leans on SAM3 and Qwen2.5-VL ([BasketEvent](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.21267)). E2E-Spot (BSD-3, with a RegNet-Y 200MF backbone) is the lightest learned spotting option, but you would have to create its frame-level labels yourself ([jhong93/spot](https://github.com/jhong93/spot)).

The defensible design fuses the two cheap signals. Trigger an attempt on the action class OR on a rim-normalized up-then-down ball path. The OR recovers putbacks that the classes miss and occluded releases that the geometry misses. Confirm MADE in either of two cases within 1.7 s. One is ball-in-basket firing for two frames. The other is the ball crossing the rim line within ±0.5 rim widths and reappearing below the rim inside the same band. Log disagreements between the two signals as "uncertain". Express every constant as seconds × fps. Pick the active rim as the one nearest the ball when it enters the up zone, and reset the state machine on hard cuts so replays don't count twice. These rules are design proposals, not tested results. To measure them without hand labeling, align clips to NBA play-by-play by game clock, which is how BasketEvent builds its labels ([BasketEvent](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.21267)).

## Slicing costs 4 to 60 passes per frame, and Kalman filters add nothing

Ball detection is a pixel-budget problem. Squeezing a 1080p frame into a 640-pixel input shrinks a distant football threefold, so each option trades forward passes for pixels. Roboflow's soccer example already detects at `imgsz=1280` ([main.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/roboflow/sports/main/examples/soccer/main.py)), about four times the pixels of a 640 input. Its ball tutorial trained YOLOv8x on 2×2 tiles of 1080p Bundesliga frames. It reached **mAP@0.5 of 0.925** (0.57 at 0.5:0.95), and most of its errors were missed balls. It runs the model through `sv.InferenceSlicer` with `slice_wh=(w//2+100, h//2+100)`, a 100-px overlap and NMS at IoU 0.1. That is four passes per frame, and the post gives no timings ([Roboflow ball tracking](https://roboflow.com/blog/tracking-ball-sports-computer-vision)). Classic SAHI with 256-px slices and 0.2 overlap needs a 10 × 6 grid, or 60 passes per 1080p frame. Ultralytics now marks its SAHI guide deprecated in favor of a built-in `tile` argument, and it publishes no runtime figures ([Ultralytics SAHI guide](https://docs.ultralytics.com/guides/sahi-tiled-inference/)). The cheap compromise is a single-class ball model at about 1280 on every frame, with 2×2 slicing only on frames where it finds no ball. Roboflow publishes a football-ball dataset and a training Colab for exactly this ([roboflow/sports](https://github.com/roboflow/sports)).

Temporal heatmap models are the cheapest accurate alternative. WASB (BMVC 2023, MIT license) is a **1.5M-parameter** network that reads three consecutive frames at 288×512. On 1080p broadcast soccer it scores **F1 88.3 at 55.7 FPS**, against TrackNetV2's 86.6 at 66.0. On basketball, where the camera "frequently moves and zooms in rapidly", it scores **F1 80.6 at 30.2 FPS** ([WASB](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.05237), [WASB-SBDT](https://github.com/nttcom/WASB-SBDT)). The paper doesn't name the GPU behind those FPS figures, so treat them as relative. TrackNetV4 adds motion maps from frame differencing but was evaluated only on tennis and shuttlecock ([TrackNetV4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14543)). Frame differencing assumes a mostly static background, which broadcast pans break, so WASB's moving-camera basketball result is the better guide.

Jitter calls for gating, not filtering. Roboflow's `BallTracker` keeps the last 10 ball centers and picks the detection nearest their centroid. Even so, it mistakes heads and confetti for the ball and loses it in blur ([Roboflow ball tracking](https://roboflow.com/blog/tracking-ball-sports-computer-vision)). WASB predicts the next position from the last three, as p(t+1) = p(t) + v(t) + a(t)/2. It drops candidates beyond a distance threshold and keeps the most confident one left. In its experiments **Kalman and particle filters "gave no improvement"**, and even its own online tracker didn't help on soccer or badminton ([WASB](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.05237)). Kicks, bounces and passes are too non-linear for a constant-velocity model, so use gating to reject false positives rather than to smooth the track. Interpolate linearly across short gaps and leave long ones empty. Capping fills at about 10–15 frames at 25 fps is a starting point no source validates. StrongSORT's Gaussian-process GSI is the principled alternative, at 7.1 ms per image ([StrongSORT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.13514)). If the drawn trail needs smoothing, smooth the rendering, not the positions that event detection uses.

## Conclusion

The main finding is that all five tasks share one architecture. Team labels, jersey numbers, shot outcomes and ball positions are all votes over a track, so one ID swap corrupts every downstream label at once. ID persistence therefore deserves the first engineering effort, and it pays back twice. Team clusters and confident jersey reads become merge constraints for the offline stitching pass, and cleaner tracks then make the next round of votes more reliable. The T4 budget and the leaderboards point the same way. Winning SoccerNet entries pair compact recognizers with tracklet aggregation. Mask-propagation trackers, VLM OCR and video transformers are slower and, in these sources, no more accurate. The free tier costs less accuracy than it appears to.

Two facts should shape how the README argues. First, published numbers barely transfer. A tracker's HOTA moves about 10 points between harnesses, and no source gives T4 throughput, accuracy for the SigLIP team recipe, or shot precision and recall on broadcast footage. The project's own measurements are therefore its real rationale. They should cover HOTA and IDF1 on a few labeled clips, per-track team accuracy, and jersey accuracy alongside the share of tracks left "unknown". They should also cover shot precision and recall against play-by-play, and T4 FPS for each stage. Second, licensing constrains the design as much as accuracy does. Several of the strongest references can't be shipped. Koshkina's pipeline ([repo](https://github.com/mkoshkina/jersey-number-pipeline)) and PL-NBA ([PL-NBA](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.19646v1)) are non-commercial, avishah3's repo has no license ([GitHub API](https://api.github.com/repos/avishah3/AI-Basketball-Shot-Detection-Tracker)), and Roboflow's basketball weights sit behind an API key. The shippable core is the permissively licensed set: PARSeq (Apache-2.0), WASB (MIT), roboflow/sports (MIT, [GitHub API](https://api.github.com/repos/roboflow/sports)), RF-DETR (Apache-2.0) and E2E-Spot (BSD-3). Reimplement the restricted pipelines' ideas, which the sources document well, rather than copying their code.
